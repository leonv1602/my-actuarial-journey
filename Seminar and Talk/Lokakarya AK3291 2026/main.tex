\section{Prolog: What is Actuarial Audit ?}
\begin{frame}{Audit and Actuarial Audit}
An audit in finance and accounting is a comprehensive examination of an organization's financial records conducted by qualified professionals. These experts meticulously review financial statements to confirm their accuracy, ensure compliance with applicable regulations, and corroborate that the information fairly represents the organization's \textbf{financial position}. This includes the \textbf{income statement}, \textbf{balance sheet}, and cash flow statement.\footnote{\href{https://www.investopedia.com/terms/a/audit.asp}{Investopedia}} \\
Actuarial audit is just the subset of audit where the object audited is mostly provided or done by actuaries.\\

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Audit Challenge}
Insurance companies usually rely on software that deals with hundreds of thousands or even billions of rows of data and there is no way one can check all the data by the naked eye. This gives another challenge to auditors where the engagement is \textbf{relatively short} (3--5 months), but need to verify all the business activities done by the company for a \textbf{certain period} (usually quarterly, semi-annually or annually). This means an auditor needs to be efficient with time, focus on high-risk items and communicate clearly what they need from the company.\\
There are 3 approaches that auditors can use to verify the financial statements, i.e., \textbf{population}, \textbf{sample} and \textbf{analytical}; and the most important item required before the audit is \textbf{data reconciliation}. \\
\framebreak
Due to the size of data, auditors need to first verify the data given by the company. Whether it is 
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{consistent}, i.e., is the amount the same as in the previous report or other documents?
\item \textbf{sensible}, e.g., is it normal to have a high amount of reinsurance contract liability?
\item \textbf{accurate}, i.e., is the amount in the financial statement correctly copied or correctly calculated?
\item \textbf{complete}, has all the component in certain accounting item has fully calculated?
\end{enumerate}
We can agree that it is tedious work to reconcile data over and over again. A particular tool that can help to capture/record the movement of the data is by doing a \textbf{waterfall  chart} or a \textit{walk} from the initial or verified amount into the most recent amount.

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{My Actuarial Audit Experience}
In my experience, the audit project usually goes like this \\
\begin{enumerate}
\item Kick-off meeting: where we align the scope of audit, the procedure to verify the numbers, project timeline and last year finding/observation (if any)
\item Initial data request: product listing of the Insurance Contract Liabilities (ICL), trial balance, assumptions and model changes documentation.  
\item Reconciliation between the product listing and the trial balance, and the walk or changes happened from last audit/year to current year.
\item Assumption checks: where we review the assumptions used for the current year calculation. The major assumptions are: mortality rate, morbidity rate, lapse/surrender rate, expense assumptions, and discount rate.
\item New Business assessment: profit tagging, measurement model used, and portfolio/product grouping.
\item Sampling process: where we pick samples to check. The samples are consist of, (Re)Insurance Contract Groups (ICG/RCG), product, and policies.
\item Model Baselining: where we recalculate or assess the reasonableness of the reserve calculation in policy and ICG level. Insurance companies usually use, Prophet, RAFM (Risk Agile FM), and AXIS.  
\item Financial Disclosure: where we reconcile the changes due to remeasurement is correctly presented in the financial disclosure
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Audit is A State of Mind}
 Sure these things are the responsible for an auditor to check and the auditor will get paid by auditing the full year data. But who really responsible to ensure the data or the calculation done correctly? 
  \begin{center}
  \textbf{The company!}
  \end{center}   
  We can agree that all these calculation or this reporting is done by human who gives input to a machine and therefore it will always expose to human error. But by having a view on auditor as a co-worker rather than a piggybacking person, it can significantly improves the productivity of the company. \\
By having a well documented workflow, auditors can easily verify the result calculated and the actuaries can do more productive work.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Accounting Standards for Insurance} 
In (re)insurance one of the International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) we use is IFRS 17 : Insurance Contract. In every IFRS, it has 3 major sections:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Scope: which items are covered by the IFRS (accounting),
  \item Recognition and Measurement: how to record the initial balance (actuarial science),
  \item Disclosure: how to present the result in financial disclosures (accounting).
\end{enumerate}
Doing the actuarial audit, means that we need to check the \textbf{documentation, assumptions used} and whether it align with the \textbf{standards}.\\
It is said that doing IFRS17 would require at least 3 parties, i.e., \textbf{accounting}, \textbf{actuarial}, and \textbf{IT}. We are going to see in this session why this will be the case.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Some Quick Definitions}
Liability for Remaining Coverage (LfRC): is an entity’s obligation to:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item investigate and pay valid claims under existing insurance contracts for insured events that \textbf{have not yet occurred} (i.e., the obligation that relates to the unexpired portion of the insurance coverage); and
  \item pay amounts under existing insurance contracts that are not included in (1) and that relate to:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item insurance contract services not yet provided (i.e., the obligations that relate to future provision of insurance contract services); or 
    \item any investment components or other amounts that are not related to the provision of insurance contract services and that have not been transferred to the Liability for Incurred Claims (LfIC)
  \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \footnotesize
  \textit{Source: IFRS~17, Appendix A (Definitions)}\\
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \normalsize
  \framebreak
Liability for Incurred Claims (LfIC): is an entity’s obligation to:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item investigate and pay valid claims for insured events that have \textbf{already occurred}, including events that have occurred but for which claims have not been reported, and other incurred insurance expenses; and 
  \item pay amounts that are not included in (1) and that relate to:\begin{enumerate}
  \item insurance contract services that have already been provided; or
  \item any investment components or other amounts that are not related to the provision of insurance contract services and that are not in the Liability for Remaining Coverage (LfRC).
  \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \footnotesize
  \textit{Source: IFRS~17, Appendix A (Definitions)}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \normalsize
\end{frame}

\section{Recognition and Measurement under IFRS~17}
\begin{frame}{Introduction to Insurance Contract}
  \textbf{Insurance contract} is a contract under which one party (the issuer) accepts \textbf{significant insurance risk} from another party (the policyholder) by agreeing to \textbf{compensate} the policyholder if a specified \textbf{uncertain future event} (the insured event) adversely affects the policyholder.

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \footnotesize
  \textit{Source: IFRS~17, Appendix A (Definitions)}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \normalsize

But how to determine the \textbf{significant insurance risk}?
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{What is an insurance contract (conceptually)?}
Insurance risk is significant if and only if, an insured event could cause the issuer to pay \textbf{additional amounts} that are significant in any single scenario, excluding scenarios that have no commercial substance (i.e., no discernible effect on the economics of the transaction)
... 
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Appendix B18}
\normalsize

The additional amounts above are determined on a present-value basis.

\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Appendix B20}
\normalsize

\framebreak
\textbf{The additional amounts} refer to the present value of amounts that exceed those that would be payable if no insured event had occurred (excluding scenarios that lack commercial substance). Those additional amounts include claims handling and assessment costs, but exclude:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the loss of the ability to charge the policyholder for future service, 
  \item a waiver, on death, of charges that would be made on cancellation or surrender, %for example death due to war, suicide, the terms that make the contract invalid
  \item a payment conditional on an event that does not cause a significant loss to the holder of the contract, 
  \item possible reinsurance recoveries.
\end{itemize}

\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Appendix B21}
\normalsize

Or as an actuary, this means to:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\sum_{i} E\left[ \text{PVCF}_{i,\text{ claim event}} \right]}{\sum_{i} E \left[ \text{PVCF}_{i,\text{ no claim event}}\right]}
\end{equation}
\end{frame}

\subsection{Level of Aggregation}
\begin{frame}{Level of Aggregation (LoA)}
In order to calculate the LfRC and LfIC, IFRS17 requires to aggregate (group) insurance contracts into portfolios. IFRS~17 is clear on how contracts should be grouped. There are three criteria:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Similar risk: product type or characteristics (e.g., premium payment mode),
  \item Profitability: expected profitability of the product,
  \item Annual cohort: issue year of the insurance contract. IFRS17 prohibits to include contracts issued more than one year apart in the same group 
\end{itemize} 
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. 14, 16, 22}\\
\normalsize
This aggregation is done only at the initial recognition of the contract. \\
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. 24}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Level of Aggregation (LoA) - Profitability}
IFRS17 requires that the product is divided at least into 3 profitability group i.e.,:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Onerous at initial recognition
  \item No Significant Possibility of Becoming Onerous (NSPBO) 
  \item Remaining
\end{itemize}
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. 16} \\
\normalsize
IFRS17 defines onerous insurance contract at the date of initial recognition if the \textbf{Fulfilment Cash Flows (FCF)} allocated to the contract, any previously recognised \textbf{Insurance Acquisition Cash Flows (IACF)} and any cash flows arising from the contract at the date of initial recognition in total are a \textbf{\textcolor{red}{net outflow}}. \\ 
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. 47} \\
\framebreak
\normalsize
There is no specific guideline from IFRS~17 in determining NSPBO and remaining category, but it requires an insurance company to assess the methodology to tag NSPBO or remaining category based on \begin{itemize}
  \item likelihood of changes in assumptions which, if they
occurred, would result in the contracts becoming onerous 
\item using information about estimates provided by the entity’s internal
reporting 
\end{itemize}
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. 19} \\
\normalsize
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What to Measure}
On initial recognition, an entity shall measure a group of insurance contracts at the total of: 
\begin{itemize}
\item the Fulfilment Cash Flows (FCF), which comprise: 
\begin{itemize}
  \item estimates of future cash flows
  \item an adjustment to reflect the time value of money and the financial risks related to the future cash flows, to the extent that the financial risks are not included in the estimates of the future cash flows, and
  \item Risk Adjustment (RA) for non-financial risk
\end{itemize}
\item the Contractual Service Margin (CSM)
\end{itemize}
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. 32} \\
\normalsize
We can re-write FCF in a more familiar term to be:
\begin{equation}
  \text{FCF} := E\left[\text{PVCF}\right] + \text{RA} = \text{BEL} + \text{RA}
\end{equation}
where BEL stands for \textbf{Best Estimate Liability}. However we are going to discuss on, which future cash flow should be included in BEL calculation, how to choose the \textbf{discount rate} and how to calculate \textbf{RA}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Cashflow in Scope Boundary}
  Cash flows within the boundary of an insurance contract are those that relate directly to the fulfilment of the contract, including cash flows for which the entity has discretion over the amount or timing. IFRS 17 provides the following
examples of such cash flows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Premiums
  \item Payments, including claims, to a policyholder
  \item Payments to a policyholder that vary based on underlying items
  \item Payments to a policyholder resulting from derivatives 
  \item Insurance acquisition cash flows
  \item Claims handling costs 
  \item Costs incurred in providing contractual benefits in kind 
  \item Policy administration and maintenance costs 
  \item Transaction-based taxes and levies 
  \item Payments by the insurer of tax in a fiduciary capacity 
  \item Potential cash inflows from recoveries 
  \item An allocation of fixed and variable overheads 
  \item Costs the entity will incur in providing an investment activity, an investment-return service or an investment-related service
  \item Any other costs specifically chargeable to the policyholder 
\end{enumerate}
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. B65} \\
\normalsize
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=green!5,colframe=green!40!black,title=Audit Checkbox]
We usually check the assumptions are consistent with the experience study and all the calculated cashflow has aligned with the product specification or not. 
\end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}
\subsection{Discount Rate}
\begin{frame}{Discount Rate}
The discount rates applied to the estimates of the future cash flows described previously shall: 
\begin{enumerate}
\item reflect the time value of money, the characteristics of the cash flows and the \textbf{liquidity characteristics} of the insurance contracts;
\item be consistent with observable current market prices (if any) for financial instruments with cash flows whose characteristics are consistent with those of the insurance contracts, in terms of, for example, timing, currency and liquidity; and
\item exclude the effect of factors that influence such observable market prices but do not affect the future cash flows of the insurance contracts.
\end{enumerate}
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. 36} \\
\normalsize
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Discount Rate should be simple, isn't it ?}
Well yes, but according to IFRS~17, an entity shall use the a specific discount rate to measure or determine specific item. For example: 
\begin{enumerate}
  \item to measure the FCF: \textbf{current} discount rates
  \item to determine the interest to accrete on the CSM for insurance contracts without direct participation features: discount rates determined at the \textbf{date of initial recognition} of a group of contracts.
  \item to measure the changes to the CSM for insurance contracts without direct participation features of a group of insurance contracts for changes in FCF that relate to future, 
\begin{enumerate}
  \item experience adjustments arising from premiums received in the period that relate to future service, and related cash flows such as insurance acquisition cash flows and premium-based taxes
  \item changes in estimates of the present value of the future cash flows in the LfRC except the effect of the time value of money and changes in the time value of money and the effect of financial risk and changes in financial risk
  \item changes in the RA for non-financial risk that relate to future service
\end{enumerate} shall use discount rates determined on \textbf{initial recognition}
\item for groups of contracts applying the premium allocation approach that have a significant financing component, to adjust the carrying amount of the LfRC applying: discount rates determined on \textbf{initial recognition}
\item if an entity chooses to disaggregate Insurance Finance Income or Expenses (IFIE) between profit or loss and Other Comprehensive Income (OCI), to determine the amount of the IFIE included in profit or loss:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item for groups of insurance contracts for which changes in assumptions that relate to financial risk \textbf{do not have a substantial effect} on the amounts paid to policyholders: discount rates determined at the \textbf{date of initial recognition} of a group of contracts, to nominal cash flows that do not vary based on the returns on any underlying items;
\item for groups of insurance contracts for which changes in assumptions that relate to financial risk \textbf{have a substantial effect} on the amounts paid to policyholders, applying paragraph B132(a)(i)—discount rates that allocate the remaining revised expected finance income or expenses over the remaining duration of the group of contracts at a \textbf{constant rate}; and
\item for groups of contracts applying the premium allocation approach: discount rates determined at the date of the \textbf{incurred claim}
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. B72} \\
\normalsize
So in fact, we will use different kind of discount for a different calculation. \\
If you remember our previous slide, we learned that insurance contract can be grouped into one as long as the contracts issued no more than one year apart in the same group, so how do we determined the discount rate since there are several cases where the discount rate used is the discount rate determined at the date of initial recognition ? According to IFRS~17, para B73, an entity may use weighted-average discount rates over the period that contracts in the group are issued.\\
It is also mentioned that some calculations are required to be calculated using discount rate which determined at the date of initial recognition, how to determine this discount rate?
\end{frame} 

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Spot Rate and Forward Rate}
We define spot rate $s_t$ to be the annual effective interest rate earned by money invested \textbf{now} (time 0) for a period of $t$ years. (Spot rates are sometimes called zero-coupon rates.) While (implied) forward rates $f_t$ is the annual effective interest rate earned by money invested \textbf{$t_1$} for a period of $t_2$ years.
Mathematically we can formulate this to:
\begin{equation}
1+f_{\left[t_1, t_2\right]} = \frac{\left(1+s_{t_2}\right)^{t_2}}{\left(1+s_{t_1}\right)^{t_1}}
\end{equation}

\begin{frame}{Bottom Up and Top Down}
IFRS 17 proposes two basic methods for determining discount rates for cash flows of insurance contracts that do not vary based on the returns on underlying items, i.e., bottom-up approach and top-down approach.\\
In practice the top-down approach and the bottom-up approach may result in different yield curves, even in the same currency \\
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. B84} \\
\normalsize
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=green!5,colframe=green!40!black,title=Audit Checkbox]
We usually verify whether the rates determination and used is already correct and consistent throughout document. 
\end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}{Bottom-Up Approach}
Bottom-up approach: determines discount rates by adjusting a \textbf{liquid risk free} yield curve to reflect the differences between the liquidity characteristics of the \textbf{financial instruments that underlie the rates observed} in the market and the liquidity characteristics of the insurance contracts\\
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Appendix B80}\\
\vspace{0.4em}
\normalsize
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Top-down Approach}
Top-down approach: determines the appropriate discount rates for insurance contracts based on a yield curve that reflects the \textbf{current market} rates of return implicit in a fair value measurement of a \textbf{reference portfolio} of assets. An entity should adjust that yield curve to eliminate any factors that are not relevant to the insurance contracts, but is not required to adjust the yield curve for differences in liquidity characteristics of the insurance contracts and the reference portfolio.  \\
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. B81} \\
\normalsize
\end{frame}
\subsection{Risk Adjustment}
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Risk Adjustment}
Risk Adjusment for non-financial risk (RA) is the compensation an entity requires for bearing the uncertainty about the amount and timing of the cash flows that arises from non-financial risk as the entity fulfils insurance contracts.
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \footnotesize
  \textit{Source: IFRS~17, Appendix A (Definitions).}\\
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \normalsize
The risk adjustment for non-financial risk for insurance contracts measures the compensation that the entity would require to make the entity indifferent between:
\begin{enumerate}
\item fulfilling a liability that has a \textbf{range of possible outcomes} arising from non-financial risk; and
\item fulfilling a liability that will \textbf{generate fixed cash flows} with the same expected present value as the insurance contracts.
\end{enumerate}  \vspace{0.2em}
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Appendix B87}\\
\vspace{0.4em}
\normalsize
Because the risk adjustment for non-financial risk reflects the compensation the entity would require for bearing the non-financial risk arising from the uncertain amount and timing of the cash flows, the risk adjustment for non-financial risk also reflects:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the degree of \textbf{diversification benefit} the entity includes when determining the compensation it requires for bearing that risk; and
\item both favourable and unfavourable outcomes, in a way that reflects the entity’s \textbf{degree of risk aversion}.
\end{enumerate}
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Appendix B88}\\
\vspace{0.4em}
\normalsize
IFRS 17 does not specify the estimation technique(s) used to determine the RA. However, to reflect the compensation the entity would require for bearing the non-financial risk, the RA shall have the following characteristics:
\begin{enumerate} 
\item risks with low frequency and high severity will result in higher risk adjustments for non-financial risk than risks with high frequency and low severity;
\item for similar risks, contracts with a \textbf{longer duration} will result in \textbf{higher RA} than contracts with a shorter duration;
\item risks with a \textbf{wider probability distribution} will result in \textbf{higher RA} than risks with a narrower distribution;
\item the \textbf{less that is known} about the current estimate and its trend, \textbf{the higher will be the RA}; and
\item to the extent that \textbf{emerging experience reduces uncertainty} about the amount and timing of cash flows, \textbf{RA will decrease} and vice versa.
\end{enumerate}
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Appendix B91}\\
\vspace{0.4em}
\normalsize
Most common techniques used in calculating RA is confidence level, Value at Risk (VaR), and Cost of Capital (CoC).
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=green!5,colframe=green!40!black,title=Audit Checkbox]
We usually check the RA methodology is correct statistically and calculation is correct. 
\end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame} 
\subsection{Contractual Service Margin (CSM)}
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Contractual Service Margin (CSM) and Loss Component (LC) - Initial Recognition}
This is the bread and butter, the totally new concept introduced in the IFRS17. Contractual Service Margin (CSM) is a component of the carrying amount of the asset or liability for a \textbf{group of insurance contracts} representing the \textbf{unearned profit} the entity will recognise as it provides \textbf{insurance contract services} under the insurance contracts in the group. \\
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \footnotesize
  \textit{Source: IFRS~17, Appendix A (Definitions)}\\
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \normalsize
At initial recognition, if the group of contract is not onerous, CSM is calculated:
\begin{equation}
\text{CSM} := \text{PVCF}_{\text{inflow}} - \text{PVCF}_{\text{outflow}} - \text{RA}
\end{equation}
If we look closely, this formula looks like the FCF calculation that we saw earlier. This is definitely intentional where it implicate that there is no profit to be recognized at inception. \\
We also saw in earlier section, we learned that even at initial recognition there might be insurance contract where it is not profitable. This only happened if the CSM is negative and it will be called as Loss Component (LC).
We will revisit this in detail later in the remeasurement section.

\subsection{Measurement Model}

\begin{frame}{Measurement Model}
As we may have known, there are a lot of type of insurance. For example, we know there is a traditional life insurance, which covers risk of death, there is a marine hull insurance, which cover the risk of damage to vessel's hull and machinery, travel insurance and many more. We also learned so far that there are some complex items to calculate the LfRC. In this subsection, we are going to learn only the tip of the iceberg, eligibility to use the measurement model, on the other 2 measurement model aside of the General Measurement Model (GMM) and what type of insurance that must or eligible to use such measurement model. \\

There are 3 measurement model, i.e.,
\begin{enumerate}
\item General Measurement Model (GMM): insurance contract without direct participation features
\item Premium Allocation Approach (PAA): short-coverage insurance
\item Variable Fee Approach (VFA): insurance contract with direct participation features
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Variable Fee Approach (VFA)}
Insurance contracts with direct participation features are insurance contracts that are substantially \textbf{investment-related} service contracts under which an entity promises an \textbf{investment return} based on underlying items. Hence, they
are defined as insurance contracts for which:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item the contractual terms specify that the policyholder participates in a
	share of a clearly identified pool of underlying items (see paragraphs
	B105–B106);
	\item the entity expects to pay to the policyholder an amount equal to a
	substantial share of the fair value returns on the underlying items (see
	paragraph B107); and
	\item the entity expects a substantial proportion of any change in the
	amounts to be paid to the policyholder to vary with the change in fair
	value of the underlying items (see paragraph B107).
\end{enumerate}
  \footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, para. B101}\\
\vspace{0.4em}
\normalsize
If an instrument contract pass these eligibility test, then the insurance contract must be measured using VFA.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Premium Allocation Approach (PAA)}
An insurer may use Premium Allocation Approach (PAA) if, and only if, at the inception of the group:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the entity reasonably expects that such simplification would produce a  measurement of the LfRC for the group that would \textbf{not differ materially} from the one that would be produced applying the \textbf{GMM}; or
\item the \textbf{coverage} period of each contract in the group (including insurance contract services arising from all premiums within the contract boundary determined at that date applying paragraph 34) is \textbf{one year or less}.
\end{enumerate}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \footnotesize
  \textit{Source: IFRS~17, para. 53}\\
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \normalsize
The component of PAA at initial recognition is:
\begin{equation}
\text{PAA} = \text{Premium Received} - \text{Acquisition Cost}
\end{equation}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \footnotesize
  \textit{Source: IFRS~17, para. 55}\\
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \normalsize
  Which is way simpler than GMM model. It is an optional for an (re)insurer to apply PAA instead of GMM if insurance contract pass the eligibility test.
\end{frame}

\subsection{Transition Approach}
\begin{frame}{Transition Approach}
It is not news that IFRS17 was published recently, and the old standard is IFRS4, so this section might become less relevant over time since eventually all insurance contracts will use one particular transition approach, i.e., the Full Retrospective Approach (FRA). However, the transition approaches can be useful if one is doing Merger \& Acquisition (M\&A). To put it simply, there are 3 transition approaches, i.e.,:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Full Retrospective Approach (FRA): insurance contract is measured under IFRS~17 since the contract issued.
	\item Fair Value Approach (FVA): insurance contract is measured under IFRS~17 at transition date.
	\item Modified Retrospective Approach (MRA): insurance contract is measured under IFRS~17 before transition date.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\section{Remeasurement}
\subsection{Liability for Incurred Claim (LfIC)}
\begin{frame}{Life of a Claim}
The direct impact of issuing an insurance contract is to pay claim. But the claim itself has a lifecycle from accident to claim payment.
The unpaid claims estimate includes five components: 
\begin{enumerate}
	\item case outstanding on known claims: refer to the estimates of unpaid claims established by the claims department, third-party adjusters, or independent adjusters for known and reported claims only 
	\item provision for future development on known claims, 
	\item estimate for reopened claims, 
	\item provision for claims Incurred But Not Reported (IBNR), and 
	\item provision for claims in transit (i.e., claims reported but not recorded)
\end{enumerate}
Actuaries refer to the sum of the item 2 through 5 as the broad definition of Incurred But Not Reported (IBNR). 
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Liability for Incurred Claim (LfIC)}
It is clear from earlier definition that there should not be a Liability for Incurred Claim (LfIC) at initial recognition since it implies that there is a claim directly after issuing the contract. LfIC only recorded after initial recognition or remeasurement. 
\end{frame}

\section{Financial Disclosure and Statement Mapping}

\begin{frame}{Presentation}
We have learned how to calculate all the need and the next step is to record everything inside the balance sheet. The next logical question is that, in the case where we do remeasurement, where should we record these changes ? 
There are 3 components where these changes will be recorded. They are:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Insurance Revenue: for the reduction in the LfRC because of services provided in the period (from valuation $t$ to $t+1$)
	\item Insurance Service Expense (ISE): for 
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item losses on groups of onerous contracts, and reversals of such losses; (LfRC)
		\item for the increase in the liability because of claims and expenses incurred in the period, excluding any investment components;  (LfIC)and
		\item for any subsequent changes in FCF  relating to incurred claims and incurred expenses; (LfIC)
	\end{enumerate}
	\item Insurance Finance Income or Expense (IFIE): for the effect of the TVOM and the effect of financial risk
\end{enumerate}
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. 41, 42}\\
\normalsize
\end{frame}
