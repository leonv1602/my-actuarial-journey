\section{Prolog: What is Actuarial Audit ?}
\begin{frame}{Audit and Actuarial Audit}
An audit in finance and accounting is a comprehensive examination of an organization's financial records conducted by qualified professionals. These experts meticulously review financial statements to confirm their accuracy, ensure compliance with applicable regulations, and corroborate that the information fairly represents the organization's \textbf{financial position}. This includes the \textbf{income statement}, \textbf{balance sheet}, and cash flow statement.\footnote{\href{https://www.investopedia.com/terms/a/audit.asp}{Investopedia}} \\
Actuarial audit is just the subset of audit where the object audited is mostly provided or done by actuaries.\\

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Audit Challenge}
Insurance company usually relies on software that deals with hundreds of thousands or even billions of rows of data and there is no way one can check all the data by the naked eye. This gives another challenge to auditors where the engagement is \textbf{relatively short} (3--5 months), but needs to verify all the business activities done by the company for a \textbf{certain period} (usually quarterly, semi-annually or annually). This means an auditor needs to be efficient with time, focus on high-risk items and communicate clearly what they need from the company.\\
There are 3 approaches that auditor can do to verify the financial statement, i.e., \textbf{population}, \textbf{sample} and \textbf{analytical} and the most important item require before the audit is \textbf{data reconciliation}. \\
Due to the size of data, auditor need to first verify the data given by the company. Whether it is 
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{consistent}, does the amount noted is the same with the previous report or other document ?
\item \textbf{sensicle}, e.g., does it normal for a life insurance to have a high amount of claim reserve ? does the change is similar with last year ? 
\item \textbf{accurate}, does the amount in the financial statement is correctly copied or correctly calculated? 
\item \textbf{complete}, has all the component in certain accounting item has fully calculated?
\end{enumerate}
We can agree that it is tedious work to reconcile data over and over again. A particular tool that can help to capture/record the movement of the data is by doing a \textbf{waterfall  chart} or a \textit{walk} from the initial or verified amount into the most recent amount.

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{My Actuarial Audit Experience}
In my experience, the audit project usually goes like this \\
\begin{enumerate}
\item Kick-off meeting: where we align the scope of audit, the procedure to verify the numbers, project timeline and last year finding/observation (if any)
\item Initial data request: product listing of the Insurance Contract Liabilities (ICL), trial balance, assumptions and model changes documentation.  
\item Reconciliation between the product listing and the trial balance, and the walk or changes happened from last audit/year to current year.
\item Assumption checks: where we review the assumptions used for the current year calculation. The major assumptions are: mortality rate, morbidity rate, lapse/surrender rate, expense assumptions, and discount rate.
\item New Business assessment: profit tagging, measurement model used, and portfolio/product grouping.
\item Sampling process: where we pick samples to check. The samples are consist of, (Re)Insurance Contract Groups (ICG/RCG), product, and policies.
\item Model Baselining: where we recalculate or assess the reasonableness of the reserve calculation in policy and ICG level. Insurance companies usually use, Prophet, RAFM (Risk Agile FM), and AXIS.  
\item Financial Disclosure: where we reconcile the changes due to remeasurement is correctly presented in the financial disclosure
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Audit is A State of Mind}
  Sure these things are the responsible for an auditor to check and the auditor will get paid by auditing the full year data. But who really responsible to ensure the data or the calculation done correctly? 
  \begin{center}
  \textbf{The company!}
  \end{center}   
  We can agree that all these calculation or this reporting is done by human who gives input to a machine and therefore it will always expose to human error. But by having a view on auditor as a co-worker rather than a piggybacking person, it can significantly improves the productivity of the company. \\
  By having a well documented workflow, auditors can easily verify the result calculated and the actuaries can do more productive work.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Accounting Standards for Insurance} 
In (re)insurance one of the International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) we use is IFRS 17 : Insurance Contract. In every IFRS, it has 3 major sections:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Scope: which items are covered by the IFRS (accounting),
  \item Recognition and Measurement: how to record the initial balance (actuarial science),
  \item Disclosure: how to present the result in financial disclosures (accounting).
\end{enumerate}
Doing the actuarial audit, means that we need to check the \textbf{documentation, assumptions used} and whether it align with the \textbf{standards}.\\
It is said that doing IFRS17 would required at least 3 parties, i.e., accounting, actuarial, and IT. We are going to see in this session why this will be the case.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Some Quick Definitions}
Liability for Remaining Coverage (LfRC): is an entity’s obligation to:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item investigate and pay valid claims under existing insurance contracts for insured events that \textbf{have not yet occurred} (i.e., the obligation that relates to the unexpired portion of the insurance coverage); and
  \item pay amounts under existing insurance contracts that are not included in (1) and that relate to:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item insurance contract services not yet provided (i.e., the obligations that relate to future provision of insurance contract services); or 
    \item any investment components or other amounts that are not related to the provision of insurance contract services and that have not been transferred to the Liability for Incurred Claims (LfIC)
  \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \footnotesize
  \textit{Source: IFRS~17, Appendix A (Definitions)}\\
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \normalsize
  \framebreak
Liability for Incurred Claims (LfIC): is an entity’s obligation to:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item investigate and pay valid claims for insured events that have \textbf{already occurred}, including events that have occurred but for which claims have not been reported, and other incurred insurance expenses; and 
  \item pay amounts that are not included in (1) and that relate to:\begin{enumerate}
  \item insurance contract services that have already been provided; or
  \item any investment components or other amounts that are not related to the provision of insurance contract services and that are not in the Liability for Remaining Coverage (LfRC).
  \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \footnotesize
  \textit{Source: IFRS~17, Appendix A (Definitions)}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \normalsize
\end{frame}

\section{Recognition and Measurement under IFRS~17}
\begin{frame}{Introduction to Insurance Contract}
  \textbf{Insurance contract} is a contract under which one party (the issuer) accepts \textbf{significant insurance risk} from another party (the policyholder) by agreeing to \textbf{compensate} the policyholder if a specified \textbf{uncertain future event} (the insured event) adversely affects the policyholder.

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \footnotesize
  \textit{Source: IFRS~17, Appendix A (Definitions).}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \normalsize

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{What is an insurance contract (conceptually)?}
Insurance risk is significant if and only if, an insured event could cause the issuer to pay \textbf{additional amounts} that are significant in any single scenario, excluding scenarios that have no commercial substance (i.e., no discernible effect on the economics of the transaction)... 
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Appendix B18}
\normalsize

The additional amounts above are determined on a present-value basis.

\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Appendix B20}
\normalsize

\framebreak
\textbf{The additional amounts} refer to the present value of amounts that exceed those that would be payable if no insured event had occurred (excluding scenarios that lack commercial substance). Those additional amounts include claims handling and assessment costs, but exclude:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the loss of the ability to charge the policyholder for future service, 
  \item a waiver, on death, of charges that would be made on cancellation or surrender, %for example death due to war, suicide, the terms that make the contract invalid
  \item a payment conditional on an event that does not cause a significant loss to the holder of the contract, 
  \item possible reinsurance recoveries.
\end{itemize}

\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Appendix B21}
\normalsize

Or as an actuary, this means to:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\sum_{i} E\left[ \text{PVCF}_{i,\text{ claim event}} \right]}{\sum_{i} E \left[ \text{PVCF}_{i,\text{ no claim event}}\right]}
\end{equation}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Level of Aggregation (LoA)}
In order to calculate the LfRC and LfIC, IFRS17 requires to aggregate (group) insurance contracts into portfolios. IFRS~17 is clear on how contracts should be grouped. There are three criteria:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Similar risk: product type or characteristics (e.g., premium payment mode),
  \item Profitability: expected profitability of the product,
  \item Annual cohort: issue year of the insurance contract. IFRS17 prohibits to include contracts issued more than one year apart in the same group 
\end{itemize} 
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. 14, 16, 22}\\
\normalsize
This aggregation is done only at the initial recognition of the contract. \\
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. 24}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Level of Aggregation (LoA) - Profitability}
IFRS17 requires that the product is divided at least into 3 profitability group i.e.,:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Onerous at initial recognition
  \item No Significant Possibility of Becoming Onerous (NSPBO) 
  \item Remaining
\end{itemize}
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. 16} \\
\normalsize
IFRS17 defines onerous insurance contract at the date of initial recognition if the \textbf{Fulfilment Cash Flows (FCF)} allocated to the contract, any previously recognised \textbf{Insurance Acquisition Cash Flows (IACF)} and any cash flows arising from the contract at the date of initial recognition in total are a \textbf{\textcolor{red}{net outflow}}. \\ 
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. 47} \\
\framebreak
\normalsize
There is no specific guideline from IFRS~17 in determining NSPBO and remaining category, but it requires an insurance company to assess the methodology to tag NSPBO or remaining category based on \begin{itemize}
  \item likelihood of changes in assumptions which, if they
occurred, would result in the contracts becoming onerous 
\item using information about estimates provided by the entity’s internal
reporting 
\end{itemize}
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. 19} \\
\normalsize
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What to Measure}
On initial recognition, an entity shall measure a group of insurance contracts at the total of: 
\begin{itemize}
\item the Fulfilment Cash Flows (FCF), which comprise: 
\begin{itemize}
  \item estimates of future cash flows
  \item an adjustment to reflect the time value of money and the financial risks related to the future cash flows, to the extent that the financial risks are not included in the estimates of the future cash flows, and
  \item Risk Adjustment (RA) for non-financial risk
\end{itemize}
\item the Contractual Service Margin (CSM)
\end{itemize}
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. 32} \\
\normalsize
We can re-write FCF in a more familiar term to be:
\begin{equation}
  \text{FCF} := E\left[\text{PVCF}\right] + \text{RA} = \text{BEL} + \text{RA}
\end{equation}
where BEL stands for \textbf{Best Estimate Liability}. However we are going to discuss on, which future cash flow should be included in BEL calculation, how to choose the \textbf{discount rate} and how to calculate \textbf{RA}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Cashflow in Scope Boundary}
  Cash flows within the boundary of an insurance contract are those that relate directly to the fulfilment of the contract, including cash flows for which the entity has discretion over the amount or timing. IFRS 17 provides the following
examples of such cash flows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Premiums
  \item Payments, including claims, to a policyholder
  \item Payments to a policyholder that vary based on underlying items
  \item Payments to a policyholder resulting from derivatives 
  \item Insurance acquisition cash flows
  \item Claims handling costs 
  \item Costs incurred in providing contractual benefits in kind 
  \item Policy administration and maintenance costs 
  \item Transaction-based taxes and levies 
  \item Payments by the insurer of tax in a fiduciary capacity 
  \item Potential cash inflows from recoveries 
  \item An allocation of fixed and variable overheads 
  \item Costs the entity will incur in providing an investment activity, an investment-return service or an investment-related service
  \item Any other costs specifically chargeable to the policyholder 
\end{enumerate}
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. B65} \\
\normalsize
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Discount Rate}
The discount rates applied to the estimates of the future cash flows described previously shall: 
\begin{enumerate}
\item reflect the time value of money, the characteristics of the cash flows and the \textbf{liquidity characteristics} of the insurance contracts;
\item be consistent with observable current market prices (if any) for financial instruments with cash flows whose characteristics are consistent with those of the insurance contracts, in terms of, for example, timing, currency and liquidity; and
\item exclude the effect of factors that influence such observable market prices but do not affect the future cash flows of the insurance contracts.
\end{enumerate}
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. 36} \\
\normalsize
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Spot Rate and Forward Rate}
We define spot rate $s_t$ to be the annual effective interest rate earned by money invested \textbf{now} (time 0) for a period of $t$ years. (Spot rates are sometimes called zero-coupon rates.) While (implied) forward rates $f_t$ is the annual effective interest rate earned by money invested \textbf{$t_1$} for a period of $t_2$ years.
Mathematically we can formulate this to:
\begin{equation}
1+f_{\left[t_1, t_2\right]} = \frac{\left(1+s_{t_2}\right)^{t_2}}{\left(1+s_{t_1}\right)^{t_1}}
\end{equation}

\begin{frame}{Bottom Up and Top Down}
IFRS 17 proposes two basic methods for determining discount rates for cash flows of insurance contracts that do not vary based on the returns on underlying items, i.e., bottom-up approach and top-down approach.

\end{frame}
\begin{frame}{Bottom-Up Approach}
Bottom-up approach: approach determines discount rates by adjusting a \textbf{liquid risk free} yield curve to reflect the differences between the liquidity characteristics of the financial instruments that underlie the rates observed in the market and the liquidity characteristics of the insurance contracts
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. B80} \\
\normalsize
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Top-down Approach}
Top-down approach: determines the appropriate discount rates for insurance contracts based on a yield curve that reflects the \textbf{current market} rates of return implicit in a fair value measurement of a reference portfolio of assets. An entity should adjust that yield curve to eliminate any factors that are not relevant to the insurance contracts, but is not required to adjust the yield curve for differences in liquidity characteristics of the insurance contracts and the reference portfolio  
\footnotesize
\textit{Source: IFRS~17, Para. B81} \\
\normalsize
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Risk Adjustment}
Risk Adjusment for non-financial risk (RA) is the compensation an entity requires for bearing the uncertainty about the amount and timing of the cash flows that arises from non-financial risk as the entity fulfils insurance contracts.
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \footnotesize
  \textit{Source: IFRS~17, Appendix A (Definitions).}

  \vspace{0.4em}
\end{frame}
